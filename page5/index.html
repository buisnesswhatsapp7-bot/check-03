Salary Negotiation: How to Ask for More Without Losing the Offer

The single most common salary mistake is accepting the first number on the spot. Employers expect a counter and almost always have room built in. Rescinding an offer over a polite, researched counter is rare enough that most recruiters have never seen it happen.

Know your number before the conversation

Walk in with three figures: your target (what a strong candidate gets for this role in this market), your floor (below which you walk), and your opening ask (moderately above target). Research the range using multiple sources — pay transparency laws now require posted ranges in a growing number of US states, and industry salary surveys and levels-comparison sites fill in the rest.

Let them go first if you can

Whoever names a number first anchors the negotiation. If asked for your expectations early, redirect once: ask what range is budgeted for the position. If you must answer, give a range grounded in market data rather than a single figure, and be ready to justify it.

The actual counter

Keep it short, appreciative, and specific. Something close to: "Thank you — I'm genuinely excited about this role. Based on my research for this position and the [specific experience] I'd be bringing, I was targeting closer to X. Is there flexibility on the base?"

Then stop talking. The silence is uncomfortable and it works. Do not fill it by pre-negotiating against yourself.

Negotiate the whole package

If base salary is capped by a band, other levers frequently are not:

  • Signing bonus — often the easiest concession for a manager to approve.
  • Equity grant size or an accelerated vesting schedule.
  • Additional paid time off, or a start date that lets you take a break first.
  • An earlier performance and compensation review, in writing, at six months.
  • Remote or hybrid flexibility, professional development budget, certification fees.
  • Title — which costs the employer nothing now and affects your next role's baseline.

Asking for a raise in your current job

This is a different conversation and needs more preparation. Build a document of what you have delivered over the last twelve months with numbers attached, benchmark your role externally, and raise it well before the annual review cycle — budgets are usually set months in advance. Ask what specifically would need to be true for you to reach the number you are asking for, and get that answer in writing.

Know when to stop

One counter is standard. A second, on a different lever, is usually fine. A third round starts to cost you goodwill with the manager you are about to work for. If they hold firm and the offer clears your floor, accept it graciously — and get everything, including any verbal commitments about reviews or bonuses, confirmed in the written offer before you resign from your current role.
