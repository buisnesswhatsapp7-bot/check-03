Job Interview Preparation: The Questions That Decide the Offer

Interviews feel unpredictable, but they rarely are. Across industries, the same six or seven questions do most of the work of separating candidates. Preparing properly means having real, specific material ready for those questions — not memorising scripts.

Build a story bank first

Before you prepare any single answer, write down six to eight concrete situations from your work history: a project you turned around, a conflict you handled, a mistake you made, a deadline you saved, a time you influenced someone without authority. These stories get recycled across almost every behavioural question you will face.

Structure each one using STAR — Situation, Task, Action, Result. The common failure is spending ninety seconds on setup and five seconds on outcome. Flip that ratio. Interviewers care what changed because you were there.

"Tell me about yourself"

This is not an invitation to narrate your resume. It is a ninety-second pitch: where you are now, one or two things you have done that map to this role, and why this job is the logical next step. Practise it out loud until it runs under two minutes without notes.

"What's your greatest weakness?"

Interviewers are testing self-awareness, not looking for a confession. Name a genuine limitation, then describe the specific system you built to manage it. "I over-commit, so I now block my calendar and hold anything new for twenty-four hours before agreeing" works. "I'm a perfectionist" does not — it reads as evasion.

Handling the salary question

If asked early what you currently earn or expect, you are allowed to deflect once: ask what range they have budgeted for the role. In many US states, asking for salary history is now illegal, so this is a reasonable move. If pressed, give a researched range with your target near the bottom of it, and add that you are flexible on the total package.

Questions to ask them

Having nothing to ask reads as indifference. Good ones are specific and slightly uncomfortable in a productive way:

  • What does success in this role look like at the ninety-day mark?
  • Why is this position open — is it a new role or a backfill?
  • What is the hardest part of this job that isn't obvious from the description?
  • How does the team handle disagreement about priorities?

Remote and panel formats

For video interviews, test your setup an hour early, sit with a light source in front of you rather than behind, and look at the camera lens rather than the faces on screen when you deliver a key point. On a panel, answer to the person who asked but sweep eye contact across the group — the silent people are frequently the decision-makers.

The follow-up

Send a short thank-you within twenty-four hours that references one specific thing from the conversation and briefly addresses anything you feel you answered weakly. It rarely wins a job outright, but in a close call between two candidates it has broken ties more than once.
